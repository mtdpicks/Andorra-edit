Food standards in Andorra: meat, eggs and organic food compared with the UK and Europe

By Rachel, 30 October 2026

Andorra isn't in the EU, so people moving here often ask whether its food is held to the same standards. For hygiene, the answer is yes: Andorra adopted the EU's food safety and hygiene rules years ago, and much of what's in the shops is made in Spain and France under EU law. Where it differs is in the detail, from how its own beef is raised to what isn't published, and on one additive, nitrites, the cured meats on Andorran shelves are now held to a stricter limit than the same products in Britain.

Whose rules apply?

Andorra's customs union with the EU, from 1990, covers only industrial goods. Food is covered separately, by a veterinary protocol signed in 1997. Under it, Andorra accepts meat, dairy and other animal products from the EU on the same terms as trade between EU countries, and its own approved food businesses can be inspected by the EU. In 2005 Andorra agreed to adopt the EU's General Food Law and its hygiene rules, and they have applied here since 2009, under Andorra's own food safety law of 2012.

Andorra is also linked to the EU's rapid alert system for food. In June 2026, when France flagged salmonella in a batch of Can Duran fuet, Andorra's Ministry of Health had the affected lot taken off the shelves within a day or two. No illness was reported here.

That may soon go further. On 19 October 2026 Andorra signed an association agreement with the EU that would bring in the whole EU rulebook on food, animal health and welfare, labelling, additives and GMOs. It isn't in force yet: it still needs the European Parliament's consent, a referendum in Andorra and a vote in the Consell General.

For everyday food safety, Andorra sits inside the EU system in all but name.

Meat and slaughter

Andorra has one slaughterhouse, the Escorxador Nacional d'Andorra, built in 2000 and run as a public service under contract. It's the only one allowed to slaughter cattle for the Carn d'Andorra label. Andorra's rules on animal welfare at slaughter transpose the EU's own regulation, which makes stunning before slaughter the general rule, and the slaughterhouse's official vets oversee its work. Home slaughter for your own table and the Islamic feast sacrifice for your own household fall outside those rules.

Welfare checks on farms are growing. In December 2025 the Government announced independent welfare audits, every two years, for 54 horse, sheep and goat farms, carried out by IRTA, the Catalan agrifood research institute. It described them as a first step towards a welfare label. About 70% of the cattle herd is vaccinated, and in August 2026 government vets checked, dewormed and vaccinated more than 250 cattle from nine Canillo farms before they moved up to the Vall d'Incles pastures.

Carn d'Andorra, and what grass-fed means

Andorra's own beef is sold under the Carn d'Andorra label, run by Ramaders d'Andorra, a farming company majority owned by the Government. The herd is small, about 1,300 breeding cows, almost all of the native Bruna d'Andorra breed, and its meat is reported to cover about 15% of local demand. The label also covers lamb, goat and horse meat.

The life of an Andorran calf is about as close to the mountains as farming gets. Cattle graze near the villages from March, then spend the summer in parish herds of 200 to 400 on the high pastures, at around 1,600 to 2,800 m, before coming down in the autumn. Rules updated in April 2026 say calves must suckle for at least four months and be slaughtered between 8 and 24 months old, and the meat must be matured before sale.

It isn't 100% grass-fed, though. In winter the cattle are housed in the villages on hay and silage, and calves being fattened for slaughter are given cereal feed as well as mountain hay. They're usually slaughtered at about a year old. In Britain, grass-fed has no legal definition either, and has been reported to cover meat that's only predominantly grass-fed. If you want a guarantee of 100% pasture-fed meat, the UK's Pasture for Life certification is the one to look for, and Andorra has no equivalent.

Organic food

Andorra has its own organic law, from 2018, written to match EU rules so that Andorran produce can be sold in Europe. Organic farms here are certified by inspection bodies the Government authorises, and imported organic food can be sold as organic if its standards are equivalent.

Organic farming here is small: in 2025 the Government gave organic grants to two farms, an egg producer and a winery. Its catalogue of local producers lists organic eggs from Casa Folch in Juberri and Can Sona in Fontaneda, organic horse meat from Poltrand in Llorts, and the biodynamic wineries Borda Sabaté and Casa Beal. The French organic chain Biocoop has a shop in Andorra, though it was looking for a new manager in late 2025, so check it's open before you make the trip. More on local producers in our guide to Andorran producers and speciality food shops.

Look out for two local labels too. Producte d'Andorra means made entirely in Andorra, with at least 70% Andorran ingredients in anything processed. Producte elaborat a Andorra means made here from imported ingredients.

Wild boar and swine fever

Wild boar are hunted in Andorra, under hunting rules rewritten in 2025. Every boar shot must be reported to the rangers, the Cos de Banders, and tested for trichinella, a parasite, with the state paying for the test. Any that test positive are destroyed. The same rules opened the way for game meat to be sold for the first time, but only after the rangers have checked and marked it.

In late November 2025 African swine fever was found in wild boar near Barcelona, and by late September 2026 Catalonia had recorded 396 infected boar, all around Barcelona. Andorra has had no cases. It has no commercial pig farms, so it has stepped up checks on wild boar instead, testing animals found dead or shot. Swine fever doesn't infect people.

At the butcher's: nitrites and sulphites

Nitrites and nitrates are preservatives used in cured meats such as ham, bacon and chorizo, and their use has been tightened across Europe. From 9 October 2025 the EU cut the nitrite allowed in most cured meats to 80 mg/kg, measured as nitrite. The old limit was 150 mg/kg measured as sodium nitrite, roughly 100 mg/kg on the new measure, so that's a cut of about a fifth. Great Britain hasn't followed: the Food Standards Agency says its existing limits are protective enough. Andorra has no pig farms, so the ham, chorizo and fuet in its shops are made from imported pork, much of it in Spain or France, where the lower EU limits apply.

Nitrites aren't allowed in fresh meat at all, and neither are sulphites, which make mince look redder for longer and can cause reactions in people with asthma. In Spain, France and the UK, sulphites are banned in fresh meat, including mince. The exceptions are some fresh sausages, including Spanish butifarra fresca and longaniza fresca, and burgers containing at least 4% vegetable or cereal. Where they're used, they must be declared, because they're an allergen.

We found no Andorran rule specifically on additives in butchers' meat, and no reported case of a butcher here breaking EU or Spanish rules. If you're sensitive to sulphites, ask your butcher whether fresh sausages contain them, and be wary of mince that stays bright red for days.

Eggs

Every egg packed in the EU or the UK carries a code on the shell. The first digit tells you how the hen was kept: 0 organic, 1 free range, 2 barn and 3 cage. The letters after it give the country, ES for Spain, FR for France or UK, followed by the farm's number.

That code matters here, because caged eggs are far more common in Spain than in Britain. The EU banned the old battery cages in 2012, but larger enriched cages are still legal in both the EU and the UK. In 2024, 64% of Spain's laying hens were in cages, against 31% in France and 38% across the EU. In the UK, only 14% of eggs packed between April and June 2026 were from caged hens, and most big supermarkets have gone cage free or pledged to. If free range matters to you, look for a 0 or 1 at the start of the code.

Better still, buy local. Andorra's new grant for free-range poultry farmers, from 2026, rules out battery cages. L'Ou 1900, a farm on the Montaup road above Canillo, sells organic eggs laid entirely in Andorra by hens that roam freely outdoors and eat organic feed. Casa Folch in Juberri and Can Sona in Fontaneda also produce organic eggs, and Cortal Llumeneres in Aixirivall and Adrimar in Sispony sell eggs direct.

One difference for pregnant women: in the UK, the NHS says British Lion eggs, from hens vaccinated against salmonella, are safe to eat runny or raw. Other eggs, including Spanish and French ones, should be well cooked, and Spain's food safety agency lists raw egg, including home-made mayonnaise, among the foods to be careful with in pregnancy. We found no equivalent of the Lion mark on Spanish or French eggs.

What Andorra doesn't publish

This is the biggest gap. Andorra doesn't publish restaurant hygiene ratings, so there's nothing like the UK's 0 to 5 food hygiene stickers in shop windows, and we couldn't find published figures for food inspections or tap water testing. The law requires inspections, based on risk, and fines for breaking food safety rules run from €150 to €90,000, with the power to close a business, but you can't look the results up. Public springs that aren't connected to the mains must carry a sign if the water is untreated.

The verdict

For everyday food safety, Andorra sits inside the EU system in all but name. It follows EU hygiene law, gets EU food alerts, and much of what it eats is produced under EU rules, which on additives such as nitrites are now stricter than Britain's. Its own beef comes from a native breed that spends its summers high in the mountains, though it isn't fully grass-fed. Where Britain is ahead is on eggs, with far fewer caged hens and the Lion mark, and on openness, with published hygiene ratings. For the best of both, buy local meat and eggs, and read the code on everything else. For more, see grocery shopping in Andorra and traditional Andorran food.
